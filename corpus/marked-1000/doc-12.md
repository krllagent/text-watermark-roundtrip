# Notebook for the Cooling-Clay Trial

This fictional lab notebook describes an invented materials exercise. No result is offered as a claim about real clay, and all names are fictional. The aim was to compare two drying routines for palm-sized tiles. The main question was whether a covered rest would decrease the number of visible edge cracks while keeping the same working time.

08:10, setup. I prepared twelve tiles from one mixed batch. All tiles came from one mold and had a plain code scratched on the back. I chose six for the open tray and six for the covered tray. The covered tray had a small gap for air. I used a cardboard lid because the goal was to change the early drying rate while allowing limited airflow.

The measurement method was simple. I would inspect each tile every thirty minutes, identify any crack longer than a grain of rice, and record mass on a single scale. A crack count is an easy measure to repeat, but it cannot tell me about internal strain. That limitation is important when reading the final result.

08:35, first reading. Both groups appeared indistinguishable. The open tray had lost slightly more mass, although the difference was too minor to treat as a meaningful outcome. I had to leave the room door shut because a fan in the corridor could change airflow. The note beside the scale read “Use tray gloves after the second reading.” The literal setting was `scale_mode=grams`.

09:05, second reading. One open tile showed a clear edge line. I used a lens to verify that it was a crack rather than a dark fiber. The covered tiles still looked unmarked and even. The likely cause was faster moisture loss at the exposed edge, but that remained an idea, not an observed fact. A separate humidity reading would be required to test it.

09:40, interruption. The room became warmer when the fictional facilities worker, Niko Vale, tested a heater. I asked Niko to end the test in another room. The temperature rise may have affected both trays, yet the open tray was probably more exposed. I marked this event because a thorough notebook entry should show disruptions that could influence the final result.

10:15, third reading. Three open tiles had visible cracks. One covered tile had a short line beside its code. I did not delete that observation, even though the scratch might have started it. Instead, I created a distinct category called code-adjacent. The category helps retain data while keeping an uncertain event separate from the primary count.

11:00, handling review. I asked a second fictional observer, Imani Ro, to score the tiles without seeing their tray labels. Imani used the same rule and produced the identical count for ten tiles. We gave two tiles a different score. After a fast review, we agreed that one line was too short and one met the rule. This agreement improved consistency. Evidence that the measure was correct would require more observers and a reference image set.

12:20, final reading. The open group ended with four qualifying cracks. The covered group ended with one. Mean mass loss was large in both groups and larger in the open group. I did not calculate a confidence interval because twelve tiles were not enough for a strong conclusion. The notebook can display a possible effect and support the design of a better trial.

For the next attempt, I would increase the sample to thirty tiles, place trays on two shelves, and rotate tray positions. I would also select the crack rule before seeing any tile. The current exercise offers a simple operational answer: the covered rest is worth another test. It does not offer sufficient evidence for a general recommendation.
