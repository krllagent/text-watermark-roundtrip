# Teaching Note: Let Students Predict the Result Before Showing the Graph

*Fictional teaching note: Alderwick College, the class, and every score below are invented.*

In a fictional introductory statistics class, I changed one part of the weekly lab. Before students could display a graph, they had to write a one-sentence prediction and a reason. The aim was not to reward guessing correctly. It was to make the gap between expectation and evidence visible.

Thirty-two students completed four labs under the new method. In the prior four labs, 11 of 32 students identified an incorrect axis without prompting. With the prediction step, 23 did so, an increase from 34.4% to 71.9%. Average completion time also rose by seven minutes.

> "A prediction is a bookmark for your earlier thinking."

For example, fictional student Ren expected the tallest plant group to have the largest average leaf count. After running code cell `PLOT_04`, Ren saw that two short plants had unusually many leaves and checked the aggregation. The graph was accurate; the initial causal story was too simple. Writing it down made the change in reasoning easier to explain.

I am not claiming the prediction produced the entire improvement. The later labs used more familiar data, and students knew an axis question was likely after the first discussion. Practice, anticipation, and the written step may all contribute. The relation between longer completion and better checking is also ambiguous: extra time might help, or careful students might naturally take longer.

Next term, half the lab sections will use a private prediction and half will discuss a prediction in pairs. We will compare error detection and explanation quality, not just final answers. The approach should remain low stakes; predictions will not be graded for correctness.

The material cost is a fictional $96 for printed cards, and preparation requires about 45 minutes per lab. Continue only if at least 65% of students detect the planted display issue without a direct hint and fewer than 15% report that the prediction feels like a test.

The fictional lesson is at https://alderwick.example/teaching/predict-first. Questions would go to stats@alderwick.example; #AlderwickLab will show the anonymous 65% threshold.
