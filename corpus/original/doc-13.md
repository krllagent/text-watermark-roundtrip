# A Public Queue Should Have a Visible Clock

People can accept a long wait more easily than an uncertain wait. That is my position on public service queues. A ticket number without an estimate gives a plain sequence, yet it does not resolve the main question in a visitor's mind: can I complete this errand before my next obligation?

A visible clock does not have to promise an exact minute. It can show a range based on the current queue. The simple version might give a twenty-to-thirty-minute estimate. A careful office would also display the number of desks operating and the time of the last update. Those details allow a visitor to check whether the estimate is fresh.

Some managers argue that an estimate will generate complaints when reality changes. That problem is real, but silence does not resolve it. Silence only keeps the office's information while visitors carry the risk. A clear caveat can tell people that complex cases may increase the wait. The estimate can change rapidly when a desk closes.

The best method is modest. Begin with observed service times, select a recent window, and use the median as a basic anchor. Then add a large range when the queue is unstable. The office should retain the raw count and verify the screen against actual departures. A polished forecast is less important than an honest one.

Consider a parent with a sleeping child. A thirty-minute estimate may lead them to stay. A ninety-minute estimate may lead them to return another day. The same information helps a contractor decide whether to move a parking booking. The outcome is not always a shorter queue. The result is a better decision by the person who owns the time.

Fairness matters too. Frequent visitors learn the usual rhythm and can choose a quiet hour. First-time visitors do not have that advantage. A public clock turns private habit into shared information. It cannot erase every difference, but it can eliminate one avoidable source of confusion.

The clock should have a small feedback path. A visitor could press “estimate was close” or “estimate was far off” after service. Staff should not use that response as the only quality measure, because angry and delighted visitors are more inclined to reply. It is still a quick signal that can identify a broken feed.

Privacy matters. A screen can show queue length without naming anyone. A public page at https://example.test/queue could publish only the estimate, update time, and desk count. Questions might go to queue-feedback@example.com. Synthetic notices could include @service_desk and #visible-clock. A budget note might state $800 for hardware and 5% for maintenance.

I would start with one office for six weeks. The pilot should compare predicted and observed waits, record how often the range misses, and ask whether visitors could reach a decision from it. The evaluation should also detect harm, such as people arriving together after seeing a short estimate. A sound conclusion could still reject the idea.

An office can begin with a small manual review before buying new equipment. A clerk could record the range every fifteen minutes and compare it with the final wait. The office could use that record to show when the estimate fails. A disappointing result would still improve the next trial by revealing unstable hours, closed desks, or a queue category that needs separate treatment.

The usual objection asks for a perfect model before any number is shown. That standard is too hard for a changing queue and too forgiving of the institution. A range with a timestamp is sufficient to give people agency. If the office can improve accuracy later, it should. The first goal is a visible, testable estimate rather than a silent line.
