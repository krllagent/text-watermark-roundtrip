# Retrospective on the QuietCart Pilot

QuietCart was a fictional grocery pickup pilot run by an invented shop called Brindle Market. Every person and organization in this retrospective is fictional. The idea was simple: let customers choose a ten-minute pickup window, then bring a packed order to a numbered bay.

The pilot began with one main goal. The shop wanted to reduce crowding at the service desk during Friday evening. The team set a clear measure before launch: at least 80% of orders should reach the bay within five minutes of arrival. They also agreed to check substitutions and missing items separately.

The first version took three weeks to build. A customer could select a slot, enter an order code, and receive a short confirmation. Staff used a tablet to display the queue. The fictional page was https://example.test/quietcart, and support used pickup@example.com. Synthetic campaign tags included @brindle_market and #quiet-pickup.

Launch night produced a large queue even though every slot looked balanced. The tablet showed arrival time, but it did not show whether an order was packed. Staff had to ask the stock room for each status. That problem created repeated calls and made a small delay grow quickly.

The team made one quick revision. Packers pressed `ready` when a bag reached the shelf. The queue then used a different color for ready orders. This edit helped runners identify which bay they could serve. It did not eliminate late packing, yet it stopped staff from searching for orders that were not ready.

The first weekend had 126 pickups. Seventy-four percent reached the bay within five minutes, below the target. Ready orders met the target frequently; unfinished orders mostly did not. The primary cause was packing capacity between five and six, although three customers also arrived in the wrong slot.

On the second weekend, the shop reduced available slots during the busy hour. That choice could lower total sales, so the team treated it as a trial rather than an obvious fix. Bay delivery rose to 87% within five minutes. The result met the service target, while total pickup orders stayed similar to the first weekend.

The team used a precise counting method. A runner would check the tablet time against the bay clock and use the same timing rule for every order. Two supervisors reviewed a ten-order sample. Their matching result suggested that the clock rule was correct and sufficient for the pilot, although a larger sample could still expose mistakes.

Customer comments exposed another issue. The confirmation said "wait in your car," but cyclists did not know where to stand. The team added a plain sentence and a shelter marker. This was a little copy edit with a significant effect: staff received fewer location questions, and cyclists had a safer waiting point.

The pilot cost $640 in temporary signs, devices, and overtime. The shop did not count normal packing wages as a new cost. That boundary is important because including every existing wage would leave the comparison misleading. The retrospective reports the chosen boundary so another reader can reach a different answer.

Two mistakes remained. The team did not retain the initial version of the queue screen, which made later comparison hard. It also changed slot capacity and customer wording during one week. A more controlled test would alter one variable at a time or record why simultaneous action was required.

Staff feedback gave the next clear task. Runners wanted the queue to show shelf location beside the bay number. That edit could reduce walking, but it might increase screen clutter. The team chose to use a paper mockup first. Its goal was to reach a direct answer before another software revision.

QuietCart continued for a six-week extension. The shop chose to keep the ready marker, complete the cyclist shelter, and delete an unused vehicle-description field. The next question was whether the narrower peak schedule would lead to more abandoned orders. The launch offered an encouraging outcome, not a final proof of demand.
